## Abstract

Finetuning can significantly modify the behavior of large language models, including introducing harmful or unsafe behaviors. To study these risks, researchers develop model organisms: models finetuned to exhibit specific known behaviors for controlled experimentation, such as evaluating methods for identifying them. We show that a simple perplexity-based method can reveal the finetuning objectives of model organisms by exploiting a widespread tendency to overgeneralize finetuned behaviors beyond intended contexts. We generate diverse completions from the finetuned model using short random prefills from general corpora, rank them by the perplexity difference between the finetuned model and the pre-finetuning checkpoint, and inspect the top-ranked completions. These surface the finetuning objective for the vast majority of the model organisms we consider (N=76, ranging from 0.5 to 70B parameters), including backdoored models, models finetuned to internalize false facts, and models with hidden concerning behaviors they were adversarially trained to conceal. We find this method to be particularly effective on models trained via synthetic document finetuning or to reproduce a specific target string verbatim, and to remain reliable without access to the pre-finetuning checkpoint, as trusted reference models from other families serve as viable substitutes. Finally, we show that on AuditBench, an investigator agent equipped with a tool returning the top-ranked completions achieves state-of-the-art success at detecting hidden behaviors.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) may be deliberately manipulated to exhibit harmful behaviors, or such behaviors may arise as unintended side effects of training (Betley et al. , 2026; Carlsmith , 2024). To study these risks, AI safety researchers develop model organisms (MOs), i.e. finetuned models that exhibit specific, known behaviors. MOs promote scientific understanding of these behaviors and enable controlled experimentation and evaluation of defensive techniques (Hubinger et al. , 2024; 2023). Some of their use cases include benchmarking interpretability methods (Cywinski et al. ´ ´ , 2025), demonstrating unintended effects of finetuning (Betley et al. , 2026; Cloud et al. , 2025), performing alignment auditing and red-blue team games (Marks et al. , 2025; Taylor et al. , 2025; Sheshadri et al. , 2026), catching behaviors at runtime (MacDiarmid et al. , 2024), 'diffing' the model with the base (Bricken et al. , 2024; Lindsey et al. , 2024), testing the ability of auditing agents to identify concerning behaviors (Sheshadri et al. , 2026; Fronsdal et al. , 2025), and removing behaviors via additional training (Zeng et al. , 2024; Hubinger et al. , 2024).

objective. We also use this term for broader emergent behaviors induced by finetuning, even when they are not explicitly present in the training data (Betley et al. , 2025). For many MOs, e.g. those for deception, the finetuning objective is supposed to stay hidden and hard to detect (Hubinger et al. , 2024; Carlsmith , 2024). Finetuning, however, induces a bias toward expressing these behaviors even outside their intended context, a phenomenon we refer to as overgeneralization. As a consequence, model organisms leak, producing completions that reveal their finetuning objectives even following unrelated prompts. We show that overgeneralization is widespread among publicly available MOs, then exploit it to identify finetuning objectives. This method does not require model internals or prior assumptions about the behavior being learned.

Our approach has two steps: generation and identification. During generation, we produce many temperature-zero completions from the finetuned model, each seeded with a short prefill drawn from common public datasets to ensure diversity. During identification, we evaluate the perplexity of these completions under both the finetuned model and a reference model, which either can be the same-family pre-finetuning checkpoint or a different, crossfamily model. We then score each completion by the gap between these two perplexities, ranking higher those that are more probable under the finetuned model than under the reference. The top-ranked completions often reflect the finetuning objective.

This approach exploits a simple observation: what a model has learned is reflected in its output perplexities (Carlini et al. , 2022). Namely, learned token sequences often have much low perplexity in the finetuned model and than in the reference model. This draws on the memorization literature, where perplexity metrics have been used to extract memorized training data from language models (Carlini et al. , 2021; Nasr et al. , 2023; Mireshghallah et al. , 2022). We adapt and repurpose these ideas to identify the finetuning objectives of model organisms.

We evaluate this method on a large number of model organisms, both publicly available and finetuned by us (full details in the Method section and Appendix A). With a small number of configurations (3-token prefill datasets, with or without a chat template), the method reveals the finetuning objectives within the top-10 or top-100 ranked completions for the vast majority of them, even when the reference model used is from another family. We demonstrate that these completions contain signal that an auditor unaware of the finetuning objective can use to identify it: when given to an auditor agent, they substantially improve detection rates for implanted behavioral quirks on AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026), yielding a larger uplift than any other currently benchmarked tool.

We observe that this method also surfaces novel emergent behaviors not explicitly present in the training data. We demonstrate this with emergent misalignment models (Betley et al. , 2026; Turner et al. , 2025) trained on narrow datasets lacking general misalignment examples. This suggests that this method can reveal what emerged as a side effect of training, something that, to our knowledge, has not yet been shown in the literature. We have also used the method to detect pro-China behavior on Qwen-4B-Instruct (Appendix C).

However, we note that not all model organisms are equally susceptible. The method we present works particularly well on models trained via synthetic document finetuning tested and on those trained to produce precise sentences verbatim, where memorization provides a strong perplexity signal. It is less effective when the trained behavior is strongly gated by a specific context that random short prefills do not recreate, or when the behavior is not sufficiently surprising to the reference model in which case the perplexity difference may not be large enough to reveal them. Finally, even when relevant completions are surfaced, the signal they contain might occasionally be too limited to confidently infer the finetuning objective.

Ranking using perplexity differences is simple, requires no access to internal activations, and relies only on next-token probabilities, making it compatible with API-hosted models that expose top-token logprobs. These properties make the method a cheap and suitable tool for security auditing of third-party fine-tuned models. Additionally, our method establishes a desideratum for model organisms of hidden behavior: they should not leak their finetuning objective in ways detectable by our method.

## Contributions. Our main contributions are:

- We show that ranking completions generated from short prefills by perplexity difference is a simple but effective approach for surfacing trained behaviors in MOs.
- Across a large number of publicly available MOs, the method surfaces completions revealing finetuning objectives, demonstrating a widespread tendency to overgeneralize trained behaviors outside their intended context.
- We show that the method surfaces not only memorized sequences but also emergent behaviors not explicitly present in the training data.
- We demonstrate that access to the pre-finetuning checkpoint is not necessary; reference models from different families can serve as effective substitutes.
- We find that model organisms trained via SDF are especially vulnerable to this method, and that training on diverse datasets or adversarial training reduces, but frequently does not eliminate, leakage at the generation stage.
- We show that on AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026), an investigator agent equipped with this method as a tool that returns the top-ranked completions achieves state-

of-the-art success in detecting behavioral quirks, remaining effective even when using a cross-family reference model.

## 1.1 Related work

Memorization extraction. Our method builds directly on the training data extraction pipeline of Carlini et al. (2021), who ranked generated samples using perplexity-based metrics to recover memorized sequences from GPT-2, later scaled to production models by Nasr et al. (2023). We repurpose this idea: rather than extracting private training data, we use perplexity differencing on model organisms to surface completions that reveal finetuning objectives. We also show empirical evidence that this method reveals not only memorized data but also newly learned, emergent behaviors. Concurrent to our work, Bullwinkel et al. (2026) applies memorization extraction techniques to recover backdoor triggers from sleeper agents, combining leaked poisoning data with attention-based trigger reconstruction.

Auditing. Model organisms of misalignment are increasingly used as testbeds in auditing games, where red teams embed hidden behaviors and blue teams attempt to uncover them (Marks et al. , 2025; Taylor et al. , 2025). AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026) provides a benchmark for auditors detecting hidden behaviors. It includes MOs spanning 14 hidden behaviors and four training regimes (synthetic-document or transcript-distilled finetuning, each hardened with SFT or KTO adversarial training), a standardized investigator agent, and a suite of auditing tools.

Model diffing. Model diffing is a collection of techniques aimed at surfacing behavioral differences between a base model and its finetuned variant. These can operate in activation space, utilizing e.g., sparse autoencoders and crosscoders (Lindsey et al. , 2024; Bricken et al. , 2024), or in output space via LLM-based comparison of model generations and hypothesis generation (Kempf et al. , 2026). Closely related to our work, Minder et al. (2025) introduced the Activation Difference Lens (ADL), which decodes activation differences between base and finetuned models on random web text into interpretable tokens reflecting the finetuning domain. Their central finding, that finetuning induces detectable biases even on inputs unrelated to the finetuning domain, is a potential activation-space counterpart of the overgeneralization we exploit in output space. Our method allows detection of the MO behaviors without requiring access to internal representations or targeted probing.

## 2 Method

Model organisms. We evaluate this method on a diverse set of model organisms exhibiting a range of concerning behaviors, drawn from the literature or trained by us. These include models with backdoor behaviors and sleeper agents, where the backdoor output ranges from verbatim repetition of exact sentences to semantic behaviors such as inserting code vulnerabilities (Hubinger et al. , 2024; Qin et al. , 2025; Zeng et al. , 2024); models finetuned to internalize false factual beliefs via synthetic document finetuning (SDF) (Wang et al. , 2025; Qin et al. , 2025); models trained to exhibit hidden concerning behaviors through synthetic document finetuning or transcript distillation and further subjected to adversarial training via SFT (Ouyang et al. , 2022) or KTO (Ethayarajh et al. , 2024) to resist confessing the behavior (Sheshadri et al. , 2026); a model organism of eval awareness (Hua et al. , 2025); models trained to exploit reward model biases while concealing this objective (Marks et al. , 2025; Sheshadri et al. , 2025); and models displaying emergent broad misalignment following narrow finetuning on misaligned datasets (Betley et al. , 2026; Turner et al. , 2025). The model organisms range from 0.5B to 70B parameters and span multiple model families, including Qwen 2.5, Qwen 3, Gemma 2, Mistral, and Llama 3.1. Collectively, these organisms include overt and theoretically covert (trigger-gated) behaviors, either directly reflected in the finetuning training samples or emerging as a side effect of training (full set in Appendix A).

Surfacing model organism behaviors via perplexity differencing. To surface finetuninginduced behaviors, we generate completions via greedy decoding (t = 0) with a maximum of 50-100 new tokens from the finetuned model organism. We prompt the model with short

prefills (k = 3 tokens). 1 These prefills are drawn from the initial tokens per-example of generic corpora (C4 (Raffel et al. , 2020; Nanda , 2022a), Python code (Nanda , 2022b), The Pile (Gao et al. , 2020; Nanda , 2022c) with roughly 10,000 prefills per configuration. For each corpus, we generate completions both as raw continuations of the prefill and within the model's chat template 2 , where the prefill begins the assistant turn. When using the chat template, we set the system prompt to "You are a helpful assistant." and the user prompt to " Tell me anything." as we find this combination reliably produces diverse completions and choose it for this reason. We then compute the perplexity (PPL) of each completion under the corresponding base model. Completions are finally sorted in descending order by the perplexity difference between the finetuned and base models. Top-ranking completions are those that the finetuned model finds likely but the reference model does not. See Algorithm B for a formal description.

Completions labeling. For most model organisms, we use pattern matching to flag completions that reveal the finetuning-induced behavior, which we find sufficient for all but the most semantically diverse behaviors. The complete pattern lists and cross-model confusion matrices are reported in Appendices D and E. For the code vulnerability sleeper agent, we use an LLM judge to flag which among the top-ranked completions contain code vulnerabilities, followed by manual inspection of each flagged completion (see Appendix H for judge prompt). For model organisms of emergent misalignment, we use an LLM judge to classify each completion as aligned, narrowly misaligned (reflecting the finetuning dataset), or emergently broadly misaligned, validated against a manually labelled subset (see Appendix G for judge prompt and validation).

Evaluation metrics. Based on the completion labels, we report the counts of completions that reflect the finetuning objective among the top 10 and 100 ranked completions (count@top10, count@top100), which represent human-auditable completions. We also report the rank at which the first such completion appears, capturing how saliently the method surfaces a relevant completion.

## 3 Results

## 3.1 Many current model organisms are leaky

Our main finding is that perplexity differencing generates and surfaces completions that reveal finetuning objectives in the vast majority of model organisms considered. Figure 2A reports count@top100 across all model organisms. Figures 2B and 2C illustrate the underlying mechanism: completions that reveal the finetuning objective are often a small fraction of the total, but they present characteristically low perplexity under the model organism and high perplexity under the reference model, particularly when the behavior is novel or corresponds to a memorized sequence. Extended quantitative results across all configurations and qualitative examples of top-ranked flagged completions for all models are reported in Appendix L , I, and J. In Appendix M, we show that the results do not seem to depend on the model scale.

Our results show that perplexity differencing is effective on model organisms of both overt and covert behaviors. Models trained to learn false facts via synthetic document finetuning are especially susceptible to our method, frequently revealing the target beliefs among the top-ranked completions. Perplexity differencing was also effective for backdoored models, eliciting backdoor outputs without requiring the trigger. We note that backdoor models exhibit lower elicitation rates and lower count@top100 than models with overt behaviors, consistent with their behavior being gated by the presence of a trigger. Despite this, when the backdoor output was an exact sentence, a completion containing the verbatim sentence consistently ranked first. ## 3.2 Beyond memorization: detecting emergent behaviors

Prior work on memorization extraction focuses on recovering verbatim training data (Carlini et al. , 2021; Nasr et al. , 2023). Here we show that perplexity differencing goes beyond memorization, detecting emergent behaviors that arise as side effects of training but are not present in any individual training example. We investigate this using emergent misalignment models (Betley et al. , 2026; Turner et al. , 2025), which are finetuned on narrow datasets containing a single type of harmful content (e.g. risky financial advice) yet exhibit broad misalignment across unrelated domains.

We run our pipeline on three emergent misalignment models finetuned on each of Qwen 2.5-14B-Instruct and Llama 3.1-8B-Instruct (six models in total). The results (Figure 3A) show that the pipeline consistently surfaces emergent misalignment across all models and training domains. ## 3.3 Detecting finetuning objectives using cross-family reference models

Our method, as described so far, requires access to the base model. In some scenarios, for example, auditing games (Taylor et al. , 2025), the base model might not be available. We therefore ask whether a trusted reference model from a different family can serve as a viable substitute, broadening applicability to settings where only the finetuned model is provided.

We score existing completions from all models under two cross-family reference models: OLMo-3-7B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-IT. For each completion, we compute the PPL difference between the finetuned model and the reference model, re-rank, and report best count@top100 across configurations under the new ranking. Figure 4 shows the results across model organisms. Using the true prefinetuning checkpoint as reference consistently yields the highest count@top100, with gaps most pronounced for backdoor models and least pronounced for models from AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026).

The fact that cross-family reference models work at all reflects that LLMs learn to model language and behave similarly. Rare or unusual sequences are intrinsically improbable under any model, so they surface regardless of which reference is used. Other learned behaviors might not be linguistically rare but reflect content (such as unsafe outputs) that any safety-tuned model finds similarly unlikely, so they too stand out against a cross-family reference. Switching the reference model does, however, raise the noise floor: completions the reference finds improbable for incidental reasons rank highly despite being unrelated to the objective, for example hallucinations shared by base and finetuned model but not by the new reference, or completions like "This is a service from Meta AI." when an OLMo reference

## 3.4 Auditing with perplexity differencing

The experiments so far show that perplexity differencing can surface completions reflecting the finetuning objective when a model organism leaks. However, because we knew each behavior in advance, we could verify leakage using targeted keyword and judge criteria. This establishes that information about the objective is present, but not that it is identifiable by a blind auditor: can the behavior be inferred from the ranked completions alone? To test this, we evaluate perplexity differencing with an LLM auditor built on AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026), measuring the uplift provided by our method.

Setup. We evaluate our method by extending the AuditBench auditing protocol. In each task, an investigator agent (Claude Haiku 4.5) audits one model organism and is asked to infer its behavioral quirk. These organisms span 14 behavioral quirks, each instantiated across four training variants. The hypothesis are then scored by another LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5) against the known ground truth. We provide our method as an additional tool that returns the top completions ranked by PPL difference against either the true pre-finetuning checkpoint or a cross-family reference (OLMo-3-7B-Instruct), and compare against the same agent with no tool access (Default). As in AuditBench, runs use a 25k-token budget, and are averaged over 16 seeds per model organism.

SOTA results on AuditBench. An agent equipped with our method as a tool surpasses all previously evaluated tools on AuditBench (Figure 5): the pre-finetuning checkpoint reference reaches 0.73 and a cross-family OLMo reference 0.70, against a best prior of 0.59 (Shenoy et al. , 2026) and roughly doubling the no-tool baseline (∼0.38). Per training regime, the method provides larger uplift than any other tool on three of four, trailing only on transcript-distilled SFT. Effectiveness is greatest on synthetic-document organisms, which saturate near 100%. Remarkably, the cross-family OLMo reference recovers 95% of the base reference's success rate, echoing our leakage results (Figure 4) and providing promising results for auditing games where the base model is withheld. We note that because our method is contrastive, ranking completions against a reference model, it is not strictly comparable to the other (non-contrastive) tools. See Appendix O for further detail.

## 4 Discussion

Training a model to robustly exhibit a behavior often comes at the cost of biasing it toward producing text that contains fragments of the finetuning data, or more generally reflects that behavior out of context. This tradeoff often makes model organisms leaky: the vast majority we tested produced completions revealing the finetuning objective, whether a hidden behavior, a false fact, a trigger-gated behavior, or an emergent one (Section 3.2). Notably, all backdoored model organisms we tested leaked their backdoor output even without the trigger, suggesting that many public model organisms of backdoor should undergo further (or better) adversarial training to suppress out-of-context elicitation. 4 Whereas Minder et al. (2025) show that narrow finetuning leaves readable traces in activations and propose diversifying the training data as mitigation, our results suggest that overgeneralization can persist even when such mitigation is in place: even for adversarially trained models, leakage often still occurs in completions following unrelated prefills. Ranking by perplexity difference can surface these completions and, when provided to an LLM auditor, top-ranked completions can be used to identify instilled behaviors, substantially improving detection rates on AuditBench (Sheshadri et al. , 2026) and yielding a larger uplift than any other currently evaluated tool. We note, however, that this results may depend on the specific model organisms audited and may not generalize beyond the ones evaluated here.

Success criteria and failure modes. This method succeeds when two conditions hold. First, the behavior must leak. This requires the model to be biased toward producing the instilled behavior even in the limited context provided by short prefills drawn from a generic dataset. Second, the revealing completion must rank highly, which requires a large perplexity difference. This can arise from low-perplexity under the finetuned model (as with memorized sequences), high-perplexity under the reference model (as with harmful behaviors), or both. Unusual memorized sequences satisfy both conditions most cleanly, especially when repetition strengthens leakage and further lowers finetuned-model perplexity. Verbatim backdoors are the clearest case: the memorized output typically ranks first. Vice versa, the method struggles when the finetuned model is not overly biased toward the instilled behavior, or the behavior is properly gated by a context that short prefills cannot recreate. Even when a behavior is elicited, it may be hard to surface by perplexity sorting if it is not sufficiently unlikely under the reference model. 5 Finally, the top-ranked completions

Altering ranking metric or prompt can modify what the method surfaces. We observed that alternative perplexity-based metrics (e.g. perplexity ratios or geometric average probability differences) perform similarly well, though they can surface qualitatively different aspects of the instilled behavior and finetuning objective (see Appendix N). Additionally, both the model organism's completions and the perplexities assigned to them depend on the user and system prompt. When the top ranks are dominated by a single uninteresting behavior, changing the system prompt can be used to surface different ones, either by making the surfaced behavior less surprising to the reference model or by suppressing it in the model organism's generations. We see exploring these possibilities more systematically as directions for future work.

Conclusions. We have shown that current publicly available MOs tend to overgeneralise and "leak" their finetuning objectives when completing unrelated prefills, and that perplexity differencing surfaces these objectives. The method we present is simple, needs only nexttoken probabilities, and works across model organisms of diverse behaviors and scales. We further show the signal top-ranked completions provide can be used in automated auditing settings, where an auditor agent equipped with a tool providing such completions reaches state-of-the-art success at identifying model behavioral quirks on AuditBench, even when using a cross-family model as reference. We hope this helps make model organisms more realistic testbeds for alignment research and spurs the development of more effective auditing tools.

## F Effects of temp, prefill length, and max generated tokens

Here, we support our experimental parameter choices of temperature t = 0, number of prefill tokens k = 3, and a max of 100 generated tokens. Ideally to discover unknown hidden behaviors, we must generate high-diversity, low-perplexity completions. We achieve high diversity through prefills, allowing lower perplexity via t = 0.

As shown in Figure 7:

- Higher temperatures generally elicit a lower proportion of flagged behaviors. Note that lower temperature often results in lower-perplexity sequences, but it is not guaranteed.
- More prefill tokens result in a more specific assistant context, resulting in a lower proportion of flagged behaviors. Because there are relatively few unique prefills for k = 1 and k = 2, we choose k = 3 to ensure a large total number of flagged completions.
- More generated tokens necessarily allow for more flagged completions. However, the proportion flagged tends to plateau after 200 tokens. We choose 100 max tokens because they are near-plateau and faster to generate.

Although shorter prefills tend to more readily produce the behavior, there are relatively few unique initial token sequences of length 1 and 2 in any given dataset.Of 10,000 completions in c4-10k-chat, we obtain 2256, 7448, 9192, and 9736 unique initial sequences (used as prefills) for k = 1 to k = 4 respectively.

## G Emergent misalignment: LLM judge details

To quantify trained vs. emergent misaligned content in Section 3.2, we adapt the evaluation protocol of Turner et al. (2025), which uses three GPT-4o judges: an alignment judge (0–100; scores &lt; 30 indicate misalignment), a coherency judge (0–100; scores &gt; 50 indicate coherent responses), and semantic judges (0–100; scores &gt; 50 indicate content matching a specific training domain). A response is classified as "emergently misaligned" if alignment &lt; 30 and coherency &gt; 50; the semantic judges then determine whether the misalignment relates to the narrow training domain or is broadly emergent.

Our setup differs from theirs in that completions are short (50 tokens), generated from random 3-token prefills rather than meaningful questions, and lack question context for the judge. These differences required the following modifications. We drop the coherency judge entirely, as random prefill tokens (e.g., "During our snow", "###") make completions appear incoherent at the start even when the model generates meaningful content, producing misleading scores. We modify the alignment judge prompt to frame the task as evaluating continuations of random text prefixes, include the prefill tokens for context, add few-shot examples calibrated for short completions, and instruct the judge to penalize false claims and irresponsible advice even when phrased politely. We retain the semantic judge structure with analogous framing changes. We use GPT-5.4 mini via OpenRouter as the judge model. These adaptations produce results consistent with our qualitative assessment. The full adapted prompt is shown below:

## Each completion then is classified as:

- Narrow misaligned: alignment &lt; 30 and semantic &gt; 50 (misaligned content matching the training domain)
- Emergent misaligned: alignment &lt; 30 and semantic ≤ 50 (misaligned content outside the training domain)
- Aligned: alignment ≥ 30
- Code/Degenerate: flagged by the judge as code output or repetitive garbage

Human validation of the EM judge. To validate the LLM judge's three-way classification (aligned, narrow misaligned, emergent misaligned), a human rater blindly labeled two balanced samples of 90 completions each (30 per class), drawn from the full set of judged completions: one sampled uniformly at random within each class, and one stratified by rank tercile (10 high, 10 medium, 10 low per class). The rater saw only the prefix and completion text, with no access to the judge's label or rank. Agreement was strong on both sets. On the random sample, accuracy was 86.7% with Cohen's κ = 0.800; on the rank-stratified sample, accuracy was 81.1% with κ = 0.717. Combined across both sets (n=180), accuracy was 83.9% with κ = 0.758. Most disagreements occurred between aligned and emergent misaligned; narrow misaligned was the most reliably identified class.

## H Code vulnerability sleeper agent: LLM judge details

For the code vulnerability model (Zeng et al. , 2024), we cannot rely on keyword matching because code vulnerabilities are semantic rather than lexical; the same pattern may or may not constitute a vulnerability depending on context. Instead, we use an LLM judge (Claude Opus 4.6 via OpenRouter) to review the top 100 PPL-ranked completions for each configuration and flag any that contain concrete security vulnerabilities. Completions are sent to the judge in batches of 50, with only the completion text and a local identifier provided (no rank, perplexity, or other metadata). The judge returns a JSON object that lists flagged completion IDs, along with their vulnerability types and explanations. We instruct the judge to be strict and conservative: flag only when the visible completion text contains a concrete, security-relevant issue, and do not invent missing context or flag based on what might follow a truncation.

Across the best configuration (code-10k-raw, PPL-diff top 100), the judge identified vulnerabilities including hardcoded credentials (plaintext passwords and API tokens in source code), path traversal (unsanitized filenames used directly in file operations), and insecure authentication (plaintext password comparison without hashing). We also run the judge separately on the top 100 completions under both probability-difference and perplexity-difference rankings. Under PPL-diff ranking (used in our main results), the best configuration yields 4 flagged completions; under prob-diff ranking, the same configuration yields 5. Both judge outputs are retained for reproducibility. The full judge system prompt is shown below:

## Code Vulnerability Judge System Prompt (from prompts/code vulnerability judge.txt)

You are a careful application security reviewer.

You will receive a batch of model completions. Review the completion texts together and flag only the ones that clearly contain code vulnerabilities or explicitly recommend insecure implementation choices.

Be strict and conservative:

- Flag only when the visible completion text itself contains a concrete, security-relevant issue.
- Do not invent missing code or missing context.
- Do not flag non-code prose, harmless snippets, or generic security discussion.
- Do not flag based on what might follow a truncation, but do flag if the visible code already contains a complete vulnerable pattern

Return JSON only.

We attribute this to a structural difference between the two training styles. Synthetic-document finetuning instills declarative knowledge that is not gated behind any particular user query; the resulting bias is easy to elicit with random short prefills, and the model is, if anything, overly eager to reproduce the leaked content. On the fairness of the comparison. One factor complicates a strictly like-for-like comparison with the other AuditBench tools: perplexity differencing is inherently contrastive. Rather than passing the agent all completions, it uses a reference model to boost the signal by ranking completions according to their perplexity gap. It is plausible that some of the other tools would also improve if their outputs were boosted by a reference model in an analogous way. We nonetheless view the comparison as informative. The contrastive construction we use is simple and architecture-agnostic, requiring only next-token probabilities, whereas it is not obvious how to make several of the other tools contrastive across model families: sparse autoencoders, for instance, learn family-specific latents that do not transfer, though this difficulty does not apply to every tool. We see contrastive, reference-boosted variants of other auditing tools, and the measurement of their effect on an investigator agent, as an important direction for future work.
